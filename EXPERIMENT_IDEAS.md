# Five techniques for small causal transformers: novelty, prior art, and evidence

**The five techniques under investigation span a range from well-studied (MTP) to genuinely novel (forward predictor, lookahead ceiling test), with critical gaps in evidence at the sub-100M parameter and small-vocabulary regime that characterizes character-level or BPE-1024 language modeling.** Multi-token prediction is the most mature, with byte-level results directly relevant to small vocabularies and adoption in modded-nanogpt at ~124M parameters. Non-uniform FFN width is well-motivated by converging evidence from pruning, NAS, and interpretability but untested in the speedrun setting. The forward embedding predictor and the lookahead ceiling test appear to be genuinely novel constructions with no exact prior art. FSP-BCE sits between—published at scale but entirely untested below 3B parameters.

This report synthesizes findings across the NanoGPT speedrun ecosystem (modded-nanogpt, ~124M params, FineWeb, target val loss ≤3.28), the OpenAI Parameter Golf competition (16MB model cap, BPB on FineWeb, launched March 18 2026), and the broader research literature on auxiliary prediction objectives and transformer architecture design.

## Technique 1: Multi-token prediction has byte-level evidence but struggles at small scale

**Original paper:** Gloeckle et al., "Better & Faster Large Language Models via Multi-token Prediction," arXiv:2404.19737, ICML 2024 (Meta/FAIR). The architecture uses a shared transformer trunk producing hidden states, then **n independent single-layer transformer heads** each predicting a different future token, all sharing a single unembedding matrix. The training loss sums cross-entropy across all heads and positions. A memory-efficient implementation processes heads sequentially to avoid materializing all V-dimensional logit gradients simultaneously.

**DeepSeek-V3** (arXiv:2412.19437) adopted MTP with **n=2** but replaced the parallel independent-head design with a sequential causal chain. Each MTP module takes the concatenation of the trunk's hidden state and the embedding of the previously predicted token, projected back to model dimension d. Both the embedding and unembedding matrices are shared across all MTP modules. At inference, auxiliary modules are discarded or used for speculative decoding (**85–90% acceptance rate** for the second token).

**Small-scale results (under 100M params):** The original paper explicitly states MTP is "increasingly useful for larger model sizes" and **models under ~1B parameters perform worse than baseline** on code benchmarks (MBPP, HumanEval). However, 30M and 100M models showed strong gains on algorithmic tasks (induction heads, out-of-distribution generalization). The MTP Curriculum paper (Aynetdinov et al., ACL 2025) found that small models (<1B) need a forward curriculum—gradually increasing from NTP to MTP—to benefit stably. Critically, **modded-nanogpt adopted MTP in Record #53 (December 22, 2025)** at ~124M active parameters, contributing to a reduction from 2.033 to 1.988 minutes (~2.2% wall-clock improvement), combined with untying embed/lm_head at ⅔ of training. A fused Triton kernel for softcapped MTP cross-entropy was added in Record #60 (January 16, 2026), pushing the record to 1.765 minutes. This constitutes direct evidence that MTP helps at ~124M parameters in the speedrun context, though the improvement is modest.

**Character-level and small vocabularies:** The original paper **explicitly tested byte-level models** (V=256). A 7B byte-level transformer trained on 314B bytes of code showed multi-byte prediction (n=8) substantially outperforming next-byte prediction, with **6.4× inference speedup** via speculative decoding. This is the most directly relevant result for small-vocabulary settings. No published experiments exist for BPE-1024 specifically. With V=1024, the unembedding matrix W_u ∈ ℝ^{d×1024} is ~50× smaller than standard BPE, so the memory savings from sharing it across heads are negligible—but the architectural pattern remains valid. The byte-level results strongly suggest MTP should work for small vocabularies, potentially even better since individual bytes carry less semantic information, making multi-step prediction more informative as a training signal.

**Weight tying:** **Shared unembedding is the standard design** in both Gloeckle et al. and DeepSeek-V3. The original paper states this "saves parameter budget without much impact on performance." The shared unembedding can even be frozen during MTP adaptation (arXiv:2502.09419), drastically reducing trainable parameters. The alternative (MEDUSA, Cai et al. 2024) uses independent per-head unembeddings but adds ~400M parameters for LLaMA-3B—prohibitive at small scales.

**Failure modes:** Regression on multiple-choice/likelihood tasks at all scales (MMLU dropped from 67.5→66.6 in DeepSeek-V3 ablations). Small models without curriculum suffer training instability. Parallel independent heads produce incoherent multi-token outputs since tokens are generated independently—DeepSeek's sequential chain design addresses this. The MTP heads add memory overhead even with sequential processing. In the Parameter Golf competition (16MB constraint), **MTP is not prominently used**—the auxiliary parameter overhead is too costly when the entire model must fit in 16MB.

**Expected improvement:** At 13B: +12% HumanEval, +17% MBPP. At byte-level 7B: substantial code benchmark gains. At ~124M (modded-nanogpt): ~2% wall-clock improvement. No explicit BPB/perplexity delta numbers are reported in the literature for small models on natural language—improvements are measured via downstream task accuracy. For a sub-100M character-level model, the byte-level results suggest gains are possible but the small-model regression on generative tasks is a real concern. A curriculum approach is likely necessary.

## Technique 2: Non-uniform FFN width is well-motivated but unexplored in speedrun settings

**Prior art is substantial but scattered across NAS, pruning, and architectural analysis.** The most directly relevant paper is the **Growth Transformer** (arXiv:2603.19348, March 2025), which analyzes SmolLM2-135M and proposes a heterogeneous architecture where critical layers receive **4× FFN expansion** while redundant layers receive **1× expansion** and "anti-layers" (that actively harm performance) are removed entirely. A 12-layer heterogeneous model with 9.57M parameters achieved **4.7× lower validation loss** than uniform training at identical parameter count—though this conflates architecture heterogeneity with a developmental training schedule.

Several other papers provide supporting evidence. **"One Wide Feedforward is All You Need"** (Pires et al., WMT 2023, arXiv:2309.01826) showed FFN is highly redundant across layers, achieving ~22% parameter reduction by sharing a single wide FFN across the encoder. **Brainformers** (Google, arXiv:2306.00008, 2023) used NAS to discover non-uniform blocks with varying FFN dimensions—MoE layers preferred smaller dense FFN (768 vs 3072 baseline). **MatFormer** (Devvrit et al., 2023) nests multiple FFN widths per layer but warns against "large jumps between small and large granularities from one layer to the next." **"Revisiting the Shape Convention"** (arXiv:2602.06471, 2025) found hourglass-shaped FFNs (wide→narrow→wide) outperform conventional shapes up to 400M parameters. **"Layerwise Importance Analysis of Feed-Forward Networks"** (arXiv:2508.17734, 2025) directly tested expanding FFN at specific layers while maintaining identical total parameters at 285M and 570M scales, finding mid-depth layers provide the most robust representations.

**Evidence for middle-layer importance:** Anthropic's **Scaling Monosemanticity** (May 2024) explicitly chose Claude 3 Sonnet's middle layer for SAE analysis because it contains the most abstract, diverse features. **"Transformer Layers as Painters"** (arXiv:2407.09298, 2024) identified three distinct layer classes in Llama2-7B, with middle layers sharing a common representation space but performing distinct operations. **ShortGPT** (arXiv:2403.03853) showed later layers are most redundant—removing 25% of layers from LLaMA-2-13B retains 95% of MMLU performance. Geometry analysis of hidden representations shows an expansion-contraction pattern across depth, with middle layers handling high-dimensional transformations. The picture is nuanced: the first and last few layers are most critical for basic function, while many middle-to-late layers are individually prunable, yet each contributes unique computation.

**NAS results consistently discover non-uniform configurations.** Liu et al. (arXiv:2207.13955, 2022) found for machine translation that "FFN dimensions in the encoder undergo an 'up-and-down' change." Brainformers' evolutionary search discovers heterogeneous blocks with varying widths. FlexiBERT's NAS finds layer-dependent optimal FFN dimensions. **When given the freedom to vary FFN dimensions per layer, NAS algorithms consistently discover non-uniform configurations**—strong indirect evidence that uniform allocation is suboptimal.

**NanoGPT/modded-nanogpt:** This has **NOT been tried** in the speedrun context. The speedrun uses uniform architecture (all layers identical) with ~124M active parameters. This represents a clear gap and opportunity. Implementation is architecturally simple since d_ff is internal to each layer and doesn't affect residual connections.

**Failure modes:** Non-uniform layer sizes create irregular computation graphs that may not tile efficiently on GPUs. Modern accelerators are optimized for uniform tensor operations, and varying dimensions could cause suboptimal memory access and load imbalancing in pipeline parallelism. torch.compile may handle this less efficiently. Muon and similar optimizers are designed for uniform-dimension matrices—non-uniform widths might interact poorly with optimizer normalization. Wider FFN layers produce larger gradient norms, potentially causing instability without per-layer initialization tuning. That said, **no papers explicitly show that non-uniform FFN widths hurt performance** when properly implemented.

**Expected improvement:** The Sandwich Transformer achieved **17.84 vs 18.82 perplexity** (~5% improvement) on WikiText-103 and **0.968 BPC** on enwik8 purely from sublayer reordering at identical parameter count. The Growth Transformer's 4.7× improvement is an upper bound conflated with training schedule innovation. A reasonable expectation for well-tuned non-uniform FFN allocation at fixed parameter count is **1–5% perplexity improvement**, potentially larger at sub-100M scales where parameter efficiency matters most.

## Technique 3: The forward embedding predictor appears genuinely novel

**SmearGate**, introduced in modded-nanogpt PR #130 (late 2025), is the closest existing mechanism. It mixes the **previous** token's embedding into the current token's representation via a small gated linear layer operating on the first 12 embedding dimensions. The model learns that `(token + 0.07 × prior_token)` is more useful than `(token)` alone—replacing computationally expensive "previous token" attention heads with a cheap shortcut. SmearGate is backward-looking (t−1→t); the proposed forward predictor would be forward-looking (t→t+1), making it essentially a **"forward SmearGate."**

**The Feedback Transformer** (Fan et al., arXiv:2002.09402, ICML 2021) feeds top-layer representations from previous timesteps back to inform bottom-layer representations at the current timestep. This creates vertical feedback (top→bottom) across time, making the architecture more recurrence-like. It's related but distinct: it feeds **already-computed** high-level representations back, not **predicted future** representations, and operates across timesteps (past→present) rather than forward in the sequence.

**EAGLE** (arXiv:2401.15077, Li et al., 2024) is the closest architectural parallel. Its speculative decoding draft model takes `concat(token_embedding, hidden_state)` and predicts the next token's feature/embedding through a single transformer layer. This is architecturally very similar to "a tiny MLP that predicts the next token's embedding." However, EAGLE operates at **inference time only** for speculative decoding, relies on hidden states from a fully-trained target model, and does not feed predictions back as input features during training. EAGLE-3 (2025) fuses features from low/mid/high layers; P-EAGLE (2025) extends to parallel drafting with learnable mask embeddings.

**Other related work:** Multi-token prediction (Gloeckle et al.) improves next-token loss by predicting future tokens, but predictions are training objectives, not input features. **NEPA** (arXiv:2512.16922, Dec 2025) trains a causal Vision Transformer to predict next-patch embeddings using cosine similarity loss—demonstrating next-embedding prediction viability but in vision and as a training objective only. **"The Pitfalls of Next-Token Prediction"** (Bachmann & Nagarajan, ICML 2024, arXiv:2403.06963) identifies "lookahead tasks" where teacher-forcing fails because computing a future token requires information that hasn't been generated yet—directly motivating forward-prediction mechanisms. **Latent Lookahead Training** performs multi-step lookahead in latent space before committing to a token, substantially outperforming autoregressive baselines on planning tasks (maze solving, Sudoku).

**Novelty assessment:** The specific idea—a tiny MLP that predicts the next token's embedding from the current token's embedding, used as a concatenated input feature during training—**has no exact prior art in the literature.** It represents a novel combination of established ideas: SmearGate's embedding-level gating, forward prediction, and input feature concatenation. The concept is a temporal mirror of SmearGate.

**Key concerns:** During training with teacher forcing, the ground-truth next token is available through causal attention at later positions. The forward predictor adds a pre-attention signal about the next token's identity, creating "soft lookahead" that could violate strict causality if not carefully implemented. The asymmetry between training (where ground truth supervises the forward predictor) and inference (where only the predicted embedding exists) may cause **distribution shift**—similar to EAGLE's training-inference discrepancy. This train/test mismatch is the primary risk. For character-level models, the embedding space is small (d=256 or similar), so a tiny MLP should suffice, but the prediction may be noisy for rare character transitions.

## Technique 4: FSP-BCE is promising at scale but untested below 3B parameters

**Original paper:** Mahajan et al., "Beyond Multi-Token Prediction: Pretraining LLMs with Future Summaries," arXiv:2510.14751, October 2025, accepted as **ICLR 2026 Poster** (published January 26, 2026). Authors from FAIR at Meta, Mila/Université de Montréal, and CMU.

**FSP-BCE architecture:** A single auxiliary head on the shared backbone outputs V-dimensional logits with **sigmoid activation** (not softmax). The target is a **multi-hot binary vector** a(t,τ) where entry i = 1 if token i appears anywhere in positions {t+2, ..., t+τ} (starting at t+2 since NTP covers t+1). The loss is reweighted binary cross-entropy with optional **tf-idf weighting** per token. Total training objective: L = L_NTP + L_FSP. The auxiliary head is discarded at inference—**zero inference overhead**.

**Comparison to MTP:** MTP requires k separate auxiliary transformer blocks (one per future position), each producing V-dimensional softmax outputs. FSP-BCE uses **a single head** regardless of future window size τ—dramatically more scalable for long-horizon prediction. MTP with 4 heads could not solve the G(2,8) path-star graph; FSP-BCE solved it with 100% accuracy. Training overhead is lower: one additional head forward/backward per step versus k sequential forward/backwards for MTP. The key structural advantage is that FSP captures all future tokens in one shot regardless of position.

**Performance at 8B scale (FSP-RevLM, the stronger variant, vs NTP):** +4.8pp ARC-Easy (71.8→76.6%), +2.8pp ARC-Challenge, +0.9pp MATH, +2.6pp MBPP, **+6.3pp HumanEval+** (47.8→54.1%), but −1.1pp GSM8K (NTP was better). FSP-RevLM over MTP at 8B: +3.0pp ARC-Easy, +4.2pp MATH, +2.7pp GSM8K. At 3B, DS-MTP actually dominated, with FSP-RevLM showing larger relative improvements as scale increased to 8B. **FSP-BCE specifically sits between MTP and FSP-RevLM**, with consistent improvements on math reasoning tasks at both 3B and 8B.

**Prior art on bag-of-words auxiliary losses:** Zhao et al. (ACL 2017) introduced BoW loss for dialogue CVAEs—predicting future utterance word presence to prevent posterior collapse. This is the closest precursor. Ma et al. (arXiv:2401.11248, 2024) replaced the masked auto-encoder decoder with BoW prediction for dense passage retrieval. Bottou & Zhang (2024), referenced in the MTP paper as "multi-label classification as an auxiliary loss for language modelling" (personal communication), appears to be the most direct precursor but was never published. **FSP-BCE is the first published work using binary BoW prediction of future tokens as a pretraining auxiliary for autoregressive decoder-only LLMs at scale.**

**Small-scale and small-vocabulary testing:** FSP has been tested on synthetic tasks with small "GPT-Mini" architectures but **never for real language modeling below 3B parameters**. No NanoGPT or modded-nanogpt experiments exist. For character-level tokenization (V≈256), the multi-hot vector becomes **much denser**—a window of 256+ characters would likely activate most of the 256 entries, dramatically reducing discriminative signal compared to the extremely sparse vectors over V=32K+ BPE vocabularies. This density difference is a fundamental concern: the extreme sparsity of the multi-hot vector over large vocabularies is likely a feature, not a bug, for FSP-BCE. Character-level FSP-BCE would likely need a different formulation, such as predicting character **frequencies** rather than binary presence, or using heavier tf-idf reweighting to differentiate common from rare characters.

**Failure modes:** FSP-BCE suffers from **noise from irrelevant future tokens**—it treats all future tokens equally, and on synthetic tasks performance degraded when future windows contained many irrelevant tokens. Random-skip summaries performed worse than vanilla MTP, indicating summary structure matters. tf-idf reweighting showed inconsistent benefits across scales. The FSP-RevLM variant (requiring a separate reverse language model of equal size) avoids this noise problem but effectively doubles training compute.

## Technique 5: The ground-truth lookahead ceiling test is a novel diagnostic

**Shannon's entropy estimates** from his 1951 paper "Prediction and Entropy of Printed English" remain the foundational reference. Shannon estimated English text entropy at **0.6–1.3 bits per character** using human prediction experiments with a 27-character alphabet (A-Z + space). The upper bound of 1.3 bpc came from direct estimation; the lower bound of 0.6 bpc was proved only for an "ideal predictor." Subsequent work refined these bounds: Cover & King (1978) found **1.25–1.35 bpc** via gambling estimates; Brown et al. (1992) established a **1.75 bpc** upper bound using word trigrams; PPM compression achieves ~1.5 bpc; and modern neural models approach **0.7–0.9 bpc** at character level, nearing Shannon's lower bound. The true entropy of English is likely around **0.6–1.0 bpc**.

**Oracle and cheating features:** The most relevant prior work is Bachmann & Nagarajan's "The Pitfalls of Next-Token Prediction" (arXiv:2403.06963, ICML 2024), which demonstrates the **"Clever Hans cheat"**: teacher-forced models achieve near-perfect accuracy by exploiting ground-truth prefixes during training but fail completely under autoregressive inference. They show the information-theoretic value of knowing ground-truth tokens is enormous—it changes prediction difficulty qualitatively. However, they frame this as a **failure mode**, not a deliberate ceiling measurement. **Latent Lookahead Training** (OpenReview) performs multi-step lookahead in latent space supervised against ground-truth future tokens, outperforming autoregressive baselines on planning tasks. **MuToR** interleaves register tokens that predict future tokens at sampled offsets, providing denser supervisory signal.

**Novelty of the proposed diagnostic:** No prior work specifically measures the information-theoretic value of knowing the exact next token **as an input feature** to a prediction model—i.e., "if you knew token t+1, how much would that help predict token t+2?" This framing—feeding ground-truth x_{t+1} as an additional input feature to the model predicting x_{t+2} and measuring the BPB improvement—**appears to be a genuinely novel experimental setup.** The closest analog is the well-known practice of measuring language model cross-entropy as an upper bound on true entropy (Shannon-McMillan-Breiman theorem), but that doesn't involve feeding future tokens as input features. For character-level models, this test would quantify how much of the remaining gap between model performance and Shannon's ~0.6 bpc lower bound could be closed by knowing the next character.

**NanoGPT speedrun and Parameter Golf context:** The NanoGPT speedrun (modded-nanogpt, github.com/KellerJordan/modded-nanogpt) trains a ~124M parameter GPT-2-class model to ≤3.28 cross-entropy val loss on FineWeb using 8×H100s, with current records under 2 minutes. Key innovations include the **Muon optimizer**, RoPE, QK-Norm, ReLU², value residual learning, sliding window attention, logit soft-capping, and progressive context window warmup. The OpenAI Parameter Golf competition (launched March 18, 2026) optimizes BPB on FineWeb under a **16MB model constraint** in 10 minutes on 8×H100s. Current best scores range from **1.22 BPB** (baseline) to **~1.07 BPB** (with test-time training). Prominent techniques include GEPA architecture, NorMuon optimizer, test-time training, int5/int6 quantization with Zstandard compression, Sort-Split MoE, and custom Triton/CUDA kernels. Neither competition has explored the lookahead ceiling diagnostic. Of the five techniques investigated here, only MTP has been directly used in these competitions.

## Synthesis across all five techniques

Three patterns emerge from this analysis. First, **the small-scale evidence gap is severe**: of the five techniques, only MTP has been tested at the ~124M parameter range on real language modeling, and even there the evidence is limited to a single modded-nanogpt record showing modest gains. FSP-BCE has not been tested below 3B. Non-uniform FFN width has the strongest theoretical motivation at small scales (parameter efficiency matters most when parameters are scarce) but no speedrun experiments. The forward predictor and lookahead ceiling test have no empirical results at any scale.

Second, **small vocabulary creates non-obvious interactions**. MTP's byte-level results (V=256) are encouraging, but FSP-BCE's bag-of-words formulation likely breaks down at small vocabularies due to dense multi-hot vectors. Non-uniform FFN width is vocabulary-agnostic. The forward predictor's embedding space shrinks with small vocabularies, making prediction easier but potentially less informative. These vocabulary-specific effects are largely unexplored.

Third, **the techniques are potentially complementary**. MTP and FSP-BCE are alternative auxiliary losses that could be combined (FSP-BCE covers long-range future context; MTP covers precise short-range future). Non-uniform FFN width is an orthogonal architectural choice. The forward predictor adds a pre-attention input feature that could interact with any auxiliary loss. The lookahead ceiling test provides an upper bound on what forward-looking mechanisms can achieve, framing the value of the other four techniques. A research agenda combining these would first run the ceiling test to quantify the opportunity, then layer in the techniques by expected complexity: non-uniform FFN width (simplest, architecture-only), forward predictor (small architectural addition), MTP (auxiliary loss, proven at byte-level), and FSP-BCE (auxiliary loss, requires reformulation for small vocabularies).
